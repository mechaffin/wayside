The roped-off area beside the start line is the paddock. Dogs wait here in
running order, and only the next few teams need to be in it at any point.

## When to be in it

Two or three dogs before yours. Earlier than that and you are standing in a
crowd of strange dogs for no reason. Later than that and you are the reason the
field is waiting.

Listen for roll call rather than watching the field. The order gets called out
loud, a few dogs ahead.

## What happens in it

Handlers get their dogs' collars off, sort out leashes, and let their dogs watch
the lure move. Watching is useful, especially for a dog who has not coursed
before, so let your dog look.

Somebody may check your dog's collar or ask which run you are in. That is
normal, and it is faster to answer than to guess.

## Space and manners

Dogs in a paddock are excited and close together. Keep a body length between
your dog and the next, keep the leash short, and step out if your dog is
reacting rather than settling.

Bitches in season do not enter the paddock. If you are not sure, ask at the
check-in table before you get in line.

## Where to be the rest of the day

Away from it. Crate or shade, off to the side, water available, and a walk to
loosen up before your number comes close. Dogs that stand in the paddock for an
hour arrive at the line already tired, and tired dogs lose interest in the
lure.

## Both sports

CAT trials and lure coursing trials both use a paddock, and it works the same
way at each. In lure coursing you will be waiting with the other dogs in your
brace rather than alone, and you will already have your blanket on.
