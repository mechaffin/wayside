The person sitting behind the control box at the edge of the field is driving
the lure. Every jerk, pause, and burst of speed is a decision someone is making
while watching your dog.

## The machine

A motor pulls a continuous line through a set of pulleys staked around the
field. The pulleys are what create the turns. The bag is tied to the line, so
the bag goes wherever the line goes, at whatever speed the operator gives it.

## What they are watching

Your dog, not the bag. A good operator keeps the lure a consistent distance
ahead of the dog: close enough to stay interesting, far enough that the dog
never catches it mid-course. That distance is why the bag speeds up when your
dog does.

## Why it stops and starts

If your dog loses the lure, the operator will often stop the bag or twitch it in
place to get their attention back. If a dog is gaining too fast, the bag pulls
away. If a dog cuts across the field toward the line, the operator stops
everything, because a dog tangled in a moving line is the injury this sport
actually worries about.

A stopped lure is almost always someone protecting a dog.

## At the start

Before the release the operator will move the bag a few feet so your dog sees
it. Let your dog watch it. That is the operator setting your dog up rather than
teasing them.

## At the finish

The bag stops and goes slack at the end. Most dogs stand on it or pick it up.
That is fine and it is part of the reward. Collar your dog, let them have it for
a moment, and walk off the field.

## Talking to them

Not mid-course. Between runs, ask. Operators generally like being asked, and
they can tell you more about why your dog ran the way they did than anyone else
on the field.
